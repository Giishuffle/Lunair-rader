# Hiring a customs broker to review the rule library

The single highest-leverage spend on this product. This document is the brief: who to
contact, what to ask for, what it costs, and how to phrase it so the quote comes in low.

_Research verified 22 Aug 2026. Prices marked VERIFIED were read from the firm's own
page; everything else is directional._

## Why we need one

Lunair tells sellers which US import requirements appear to apply to their products.
If that content is wrong, the product is not merely unhelpful - it is harmful, and it
is the fastest way to destroy the trust the whole brand rests on. Nobody on this
project is a customs professional. Software review cannot substitute for domain review.

## The credential that matters

- **LCB (Licensed Customs Broker)** - the only *federal license* here. Issued by CBP
  after an exam and background check, renewed triennially. **Verify it against CBP's own
  list, not the person's website.**
- **CCS (Certified Customs Specialist)** - an NCBFAA private certification, not a
  license. Weaker than it sounds: LCBs are grandfathered in without course or exam, so
  "LCB, CCS" is largely one credential wearing two hats.
- **CES (Certified Export Specialist)** - export-side. **Irrelevant to us.** Do not pay
  a premium for it.
- Ideal profile: **LCB with demonstrable experience filing with the partner government
  agencies we cover** - FDA, CPSC, FCC, DOT/PHMSA. A trade attorney who is *also* an LCB
  is the premium version.

## What it actually costs - corrected

My earlier $500-$1,500 estimate was too low for a full library audit. The market
benchmark for a classification review of a small product line is **$5,000-$15,000**, and
independent consultants bill **$150-$400/hour**. A $3,000 budget buys roughly 8 to 15
consultant hours, which is not a full audit.

Three structures that genuinely fit the budget, cheapest first:

1. **$495-$1,500** - two or three USA Customs Clearance sessions, each scoped to one
   product category, used to pressure-test the riskiest rules. Start here.
2. **$2,000-$3,000** - eight to twelve hours from an hourly LCB as a red-team pass over
   the whole library with written comments. Best coverage per dollar.
3. **$2,500-$3,000** - a capped fixed-fee "accuracy review, no opinion letter" from
   Braumiller or ITC-Diligence.

## How to phrase it so the quote comes in low

This is the most valuable tactic in this document. **Most firms will resist putting
their name on consumer-facing regulatory content without a liability carve-out, and
they price that risk into the quote.** So:

- Ask for an **internal accuracy review with written comments, no published
  attribution**. Say explicitly that you are not asking them to certify anything and
  that their name will not appear anywhere.
- Ask for a **capped fixed fee for a document review**, not a classification project.
  The word "classification" triggers the $5k-15k pricing band; "review" does not.
- Only after the review is delivered and you know the relationship works, open a
  separate conversation about whether they would let you name them publicly. That is
  worth more than the review itself, but asking up front raises the price of both.

## The scope to send

> **Engagement: internal accuracy review of an import-requirements reference library**
>
> We publish a subscription information service that shows US e-commerce sellers which
> import requirements appear to apply to their products, based on a description they
> provide. We are not a broker, we file nothing, and we give no advice - every item
> links its official government source and users self-certify their own status.
>
> We would like an internal review of our requirement library before launch. Your name
> would not appear anywhere in the product or our marketing, and we are not asking you
> to certify or approve anything. Specifically:
>
> 1. **Accuracy.** For each product category, are the listed requirements correct, and
>    is anything materially missing?
> 2. **Applicability logic.** We attach requirements using simple conditions - product
>    category, contains a battery, intended for children, country of origin. Are those
>    triggers right, and where are they too broad or too narrow?
> 3. **Wording.** Our text is deliberately plain-English and hedged ("appears to apply",
>    never "you must"). Does any wording overstate certainty or stray toward advice?
> 4. **Presentation of classification.** We show two or three candidate HTS codes and
>    require the user to select and confirm their own. Is that presented responsibly,
>    and how would you word that screen?
> 5. **Blind spots.** What do small importers most commonly get wrong that our library
>    does not cover at all?
>
> Deliverable: written comments plus a one-hour call. We would like a capped fixed fee
> for this scope.

## What to send them

- The rule library exported as a readable document - one section per category, with the
  plain-English text, the trigger conditions, and the source links.
- The "Product posture" section of `docs/legal/tos-outline.md`, which was written for
  exactly this audience.
- Two or three sample product profiles with the output we would show, so they see the
  end result rather than only the raw rules.

## Scope discipline

Review only the **5 to 8 categories we intend to launch with**, not all 20. Lower quote,
faster answer, and it tells us whether this reviewer is a good fit before committing.

## Afterwards

A broker who likes the product is a natural first **Lighthouse white-label partner** -
they could resell monitoring to their own client book. Raise it after the review is
done, alongside the attribution question, never before.
